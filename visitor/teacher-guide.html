A short worked answer

Why the prime assumption helps

The lesson starts with one failed rule, adds a prime assumption, then asks learners to reuse the idea for a product.

1. Check the counterexample by hand

Two squared is four. Four goes into four exactly once. Four does not go into two a whole number of times. So at n = 2 the rule fails. That disproves the unrestricted claim that a divisor of a square must divide its root.

4 goes into 2 × 2 exactly; 4 does not go into 2 evenly.

One counterexample is enough to disprove that unrestricted statement. It does not show that every composite divisor fails.

2. Use primality twice

For the transfer question, suppose a prime number p divides the square of a × b.

  1. Apply the prime-divides-a-square fact to a × b. Now p divides a × b.
  2. Since p is prime, a prime that divides a product divides at least one factor. Thus p divides a or p divides b.

This is a known elementary argument. The saved exercise is a teaching example, not a new theorem or a measured model-evaluation result.

3. Keep the two claims separate

The square claim has a useful composite example: if 6 divides n squared, then both 2 and 3 divide n; because 2 and 3 are coprime, 6 divides n.

That does not make 6 behave like a prime in the product claim. With a = 2 and b = 3, 6 divides a × b, but it divides neither factor. This is why the lesson uses a prime assumption for the product step.

Optional Lean proof and exact statement

The target is: if a natural prime p divides (a*b)^2, then it divides a or b.

theorem prime_dvd_factor_of_product_square (p a b : Nat)
    (hp : p.Prime) (h : p ∣ (a * b) ^ 2) : p ∣ a ∨ p ∣ b := by
  have hProduct : p ∣ a * b := hp.dvd_of_dvd_pow h
  exact hp.dvd_mul.mp hProduct

The first line uses primality to move from a divisor of a square to a divisor of its base. The second uses the prime-divides-product property.

Try it with a learner

  1. Ask them to verify 4 and 2 without opening the proof.
  2. Ask which assumption changed when the prime statement is introduced.
  3. Give the product question and ask them to name the two steps before writing code.
