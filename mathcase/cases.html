Number theoryWhen a prime divides a square, what else must it divide?

Start with a small number, then follow a worked lesson about why the divisor matters. Lean, a program that checks proofs, accepted the corrected argument for every prime divisor. The example with 4 and 2 is a separate arithmetic counterexample.

Read the same proof at five levels

These explanations were written for this page. Each explains the same statement. Lean checked the saved formal proof. The five explanations have not had independent teaching review.

High school · Think of a square as a product

A square is a number times itself. A prime that divides a product must divide at least one of its factors. In a square the two factors are the same number, so it must divide that number. For example, 5 divides 100 = 10 × 10, and it divides 10.

Associate · Use the prime product rule

Write n² as n × n. Euclid’s lemma says that if a prime p divides a product, then p divides its first factor or its second factor. Either conclusion here is p ∣ n.

Bachelor’s · Look at the remainder

Reduce n modulo p. If its remainder were nonzero, it would have a multiplicative inverse because p is prime. Its square could not be zero, since multiplying by the inverse twice would give 1 = 0 modulo p. So n has remainder zero.

Master’s · Recognize a prime ideal

In the integers, the ideal (p) of multiples of a prime p is a prime ideal. Since n · n belongs to (p), one of its factors belongs to (p). Both factors are n, which gives p ∣ n.

Doctorate · Reframe it in the quotient

The image of n in ℤ/(p) has square zero. This quotient is a field and hence reduced, so the image of n is zero. The square argument uses only the absence of nonzero square-zero elements in the quotient.

Logic & discrete structuresWhat does it mean to belong to a two-item list?

A value belongs to [b, c] exactly when it equals b or equals c. For example, 5 belongs to [2, 5] because it equals the second entry. The prototype records five draft explanations of this known theorem, from high school through doctorate. Independent teaching review remains open.

Can a small Ising grid have a smooth count profile?
Ising · exact finite computation · counterexample found

Plain question: if we fix how many of the nine sites point up, do the numbers of arrangements with 8, 10, 12 broken neighboring links change in a smoothly log-concave way?

On the periodic 3×3 grid, fixing four up sites gives the exact counts 45, 36, 45. Smooth log-concavity would require 36² ≥ 45×45, but 1,296 < 2,025. The same profile appears for five up sites by flipping every spin.

Every one of the 2⁹ = 512 labeled arrangements was checked two ways: direct edge enumeration and an independent eight-state column-transfer calculation. The other eight fixed-up-spin profiles passed this finite test.

This is a bounded computation on one small torus. It is not a general Ising theorem, a thermodynamic-limit claim, or a novelty claim.

Plain claim: repeat this shortcut 22 times: halve even numbers; for odd numbers, do 3n+1 and halve. For every start n ≥ 3 whose shortcut path begins on an odd number at most 13 times, a regular Collatz step takes it below its start by step 35.

The Lean-checked count is 3,593,934 of 4,194,304 possible 22-step parity patterns (85.6861%).

Example: starting at 7, the shortcut path begins 7 → 11 → 17 → 26 → 13 → 20 → 10 → 5 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1. This is one example, not the proof. Can a number fall below itself within 44 steps?
Collatz · Lean checked

The rule: halve an even number. For an odd number, triple it, add 1, and halve once. Repeat 27 times.

Plain claim: for every start n ≥ 26,018 with at most 17 odd moves, the ordinary Collatz value at step 27+h is below its start, no later than step 44.

Example: 26,018 has 12 odd moves and falls below its start after 39 ordinary steps, reaching 107. That example illustrates the rule; it is not the proof.

The rule covers only starts that meet its size and parity conditions. It does not prove that every Collatz sequence reaches 1.
