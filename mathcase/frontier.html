Frontier mathematics

Which patterns can you avoid?

Color numbers on a line, join dots with colored edges, or split a fraction into smaller pieces. A small example gives you the rules of each puzzle. The six main checks below, plus five bounded C7 code-search results, show what happens when you take those rules further.

Number patterns · two colors

Can two colors avoid seven equally spaced numbers of one color?

Write 2, 5, 8. The gap is always 3. Keep going and you get 11, 14, 17, 20. Coloring all seven blue would make exactly the pattern you want to avoid.

A known coloring assigns two colors to 1 through 3,703 and avoids every such seven-number pattern. MathCase checked it. One entire family of ways to color 3,704 positions failed. Other families are still possible.

Inspect the exact result, its limits and sources

These numbers can skip positions: for example, 2, 5, and 8 are equally spaced. A checked two-coloring covers 3,703 positions without a single-color pattern of seven. That reproduces the known lower bound W(2,7) ≥ 3,704.

MathCase also checked 617 cases in one specified quadratic-residue construction family at length 3,704, covering 644,907,008 assignments. That family is ruled out; other colorings are not.

Next question. check a different construction family, or produce a general certificate covering every coloring at length 3,704.

Number patterns · five colors

Can five colors avoid even a three-number pattern?

Give 2 and 5 the same color. Now 8 needs a different color because the gaps in 2, 5, 8 are equal. That is just one of the triples a coloring must handle.

A checked coloring works through 170. A checked coloring at 171 or a proof ruling all such colorings out would settle the next step.

Inspect the exact result, its limits and sources

A checked coloring covers 170 positions without a monochromatic three-term progression, so W(5,3) ≥ 171. The unrestricted 171-position search has 855 variables and 38,006 clauses.

Addition · five colors

Where can you put 208 without creating a same-color sum?

If 2 and 3 are blue, 5 cannot also be blue, because 2 + 3 = 5. Only sums of two distinct positive numbers count.

Two known five-color arrangements cover 1 through 207. Starting from either one, MathCase tried every color for 208 and every change of up to 18 earlier entries; neither small neighborhood extended it. A larger rearrangement or a different starting arrangement could still work.

A separate saved arrangement of 1 through 208 fails at one sum: 4 + 8 = 12. Changing one, two, or three entries cannot repair that arrangement. A different arrangement or a larger repair may still work.

A newer block-crossover pilot mixed pieces of the two published 207 arrangements and tried 39 starts in 30 seconds. Its best candidate still had one conflict, 12 + 196 = 208 in one color. It is a distinct near miss, not a 208-coloring and not an impossibility proof.

Inspect the exact result, its limits and sources

The rule forbids same-color sums a + b = c when a and b are distinct positive numbers. Two known colorings cover 1 through 207; MathCase checked both and searched every color for 208 while changing up to 18 old entries.

All ten searches around the two published colorings finished. Starting from either one, at least 19 old positions must change before an extension can work. The separate one-conflict arrangement was also checked: all 4,064,044 possible one-, two-, and three-entry recolorings that could break 4 + 8 = 12 were exhausted. The best still had 9, 8, and 7 forbidden sums at those respective distances.

The one-conflict result applies to that exact saved arrangement. It does not rule out another valid arrangement of 1 through 208 or establish that 208 is impossible.

These saved local-repair probes are secondary diagnostics, not certificates and not claims that 208 is impossible. They apply only to the exact saved coloring and edit limit.

Graphs · four colors

Can 51 dots avoid a one-color triangle?

Draw three dots and join each pair. Three red lines give a red triangle. Change any one line to blue and that triangle disappears. With many dots, that change affects other triangles too.

A known four-color drawing on 50 dots passes the triangle check. Keeping those lines fixed, every way of adding a 51st dot creates a one-color triangle. Even changing one or two old lines is insufficient for this drawing.

A known graph on 50 vertices has no monochromatic triangle, giving R(3,3,3,3) ≥ 51. MathCase checked every triangle and every way to attach a 51st vertex while leaving the old graph unchanged.

A second bounded check found that an extension from this particular starting graph needs at least three old edges recolored. The published interval is 51–60; MathCase did not reproduce the upper-bound proof.

Graphs · two colors

Can you add a 102nd dot without making a one-color group of six?

Six dots have 15 lines between them. If all 15 are red, those dots form a red group of six. The same test applies to blue.

A known drawing on 101 dots avoids both kinds of group. Adding a dot while keeping the old coloring fails. A checked restriction says that any extension of this particular drawing must change at least 16 old lines.

Inspect the exact result, its limits and sources

The known Paley graph on 101 vertices has no monochromatic six-clique, giving R(6,6) ≥ 102. A checked certificate rules out adding one new vertex while keeping every old edge the same.

A fresh local Lean replay rebuilt the three MathCase proof files from source under the current pinned dependencies. An anonymous public-clone replay also passed all five setup, build, and audit commands in 1,319 seconds. It confirms this known lower bound; it does not add a new one or replace independent human review. In this release, a stranger has not yet reproduced the run independently.

Other bounded checks find that at least 16 old edges must change. None of 33 predefined structured edge-change patterns gave a valid extension; each produced a six-clique. These restrictions apply to this starting graph and these changes only.

Next question. find a materially different 102-vertex coloring and check every six-vertex subset.

Fractions · exact identities

Can you write 4/21 as three fractions with 1 on top?

Try 1/6 + 1/63 + 1/126. In parts of size 1/126, the sum is 21 + 2 + 1 = 24 parts. And 24/126 is exactly 4/21.

Lean checked two formulas that work for denominators 21, 49, 77 and every later number obtained by adding 28. Another formula covers all positive multiples of seven. The full Erdős–Straus question asks for every integer denominator of at least 2.

A separate finite replay of Dahan's two-parameter criterion succeeds on 1,000,000 eligible primes with n ≡ 1 (mod 24), with a separate row-by-row audit. That is a larger checked sample, not an asymptotic proof.

Codes · five-symbol words

Can 368 five-symbol words avoid every forbidden pair?

Two words conflict when every coordinate differs by 0, 1, or 6 modulo 7. A valid code keeps every pair out of that conflict pattern.

A bounded whole-graph search started from an empty set and tried 12,690 randomized greedy constructions. Its best valid set had 253 words. A separate 120-second multi-ejection run started from the known 367-word code plus eight independently checked one-swap variants; it made 9,941 moves across 83 starts and still found no 368-word code or new equal-size seed. A later 60-second continuation prepared nine validated 367-word seeds and made 455 coordinated moves; it also stayed at 367. The known 367-word construction still passes the independent checker.

Inspect the exact result, its limits and sources

The run ended NO_368_FOUND_UNKNOWN. A planted conflicting pair was rejected by the separate checker, which also checked all 67,161 pairs in the known 367-word control. This is a search miss, not a proof that 368 is impossible and not a change to the known 367 ≤ α(C7⁵) ≤ 401 interval.

Next question. try a construction family outside these known-seed neighborhoods; neither bounded search proves that 368 is impossible.

Grid physics · a finite check

Does a tiny Ising grid keep a smooth counting pattern?

Take a 3×3 square whose edges wrap around. Count arrangements with a fixed number of up spins, then group them by how many edges are “broken” between unlike neighbors.

For the tested four-up and five-up profiles, the three interior counts are 45, 36, 45. Because 36² = 1,296 is less than 45 × 45 = 2,025, those two profiles fail the log-concavity test. The other eight tested profiles pass. Separate width-four and width-five transfer checks agree with direct enumeration on their listed finite lengths. A 6×3 check also agrees across three methods: direct enumeration of all 262,144 spin assignments, a 64-state transfer calculation, and an independent Gray-code walk. Of the 19 fixed-up-spin profiles, 9 fail the test; the first failure has counts 36, 162, 972, so 162² = 26,244 < 34,992 = 36×972. A new 7×3 check agrees across direct Gray-code enumeration and an eight-state transfer calculation for all 2,097,152 assignments; 14 of 22 profiles fail, first at 63, 315, 1,680.

Inspect the exact finite result and its limits

MathCase computed the profiles two ways: direct enumeration of all spin arrangements and an independent transfer calculation. Both methods agree on every tested bin. Leading and trailing zero bins were trimmed; interior zero bins were retained, so the test does not hide a zero.

This is a result about the tested small periodic grids and finite profiles only. It is not a theorem about larger grids, the thermodynamic limit, or every Ising model.
