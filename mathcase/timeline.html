MathCase · research timeline

How did the checks change over time?

In October, a claim about divisibility showed why one word can matter. Four divides 2 squared, yet four does not divide 2. By November the project was checking whole families of starting numbers and asking whether someone else could repeat the work.

From a single example to families of numbers

The dates below follow the research from October through November 28, 2026. Some entries span a month.

  1. October 2026

    Divisibility · checked proof

    What changes when the divisor is prime?

    If a prime divides a square, it divides the number being squared. Lean rejected the first proof attempt and accepted the repair. The example with 4 and 2 was a separate arithmetic check of what can go wrong with a composite divisor.

    Read the original evidence and scope

    MathCase preserved a candidate that Lean rejected, then the corrected proof that Lean accepted for the exact prime statement. A separate composite example shows why the primality condition matters.

    Established: the saved checker path can retain and display a failed attempt beside a passing proof for a known result. Not established: a new theorem or broad open-problem solving ability.

  2. October–November 2026

    Collatz · bounded descent

    Which starts are guaranteed to drop?

    Group numbers by the remainder they leave after division. One Lean proof covered 7,825 of the 8,192 remainder groups it considered. A separate, larger count found a recorded drop for 1,021,248 of 1,048,576 groups.

    Read the original evidence and scope

    The public record includes a Lean source covering 7,825 of 8,192 residue classes within 21 steps. A larger chain reports 1,021,248 of 1,048,576 classes with a listed bounded descent.

    Established: finite coverage counts and selected bounded proofs, with their separate evidence types. Not established: that every integer descends or that the Collatz conjecture is solved.

    Read the bounded Collatz records →
  3. November 12–13, 2026

    Collatz · assembling a proof

    Can thousands of number families share a proof?

    Start at 239. After 32 ordinary Collatz moves you are at 122. The team assembled a proof that identifies the exact smaller endpoint for every start in 2,652 listed families.

    Read the original evidence and scope

    The preserved record covers 229 shard modules. The 2²⁰ result combines 133 intermediate modules with the existing final composition theorem; earlier all-at-once and elaboration attempts remain in the history.

    Established: recorded source, receipt, and bounded theorem evidence. Not established: a global Collatz result or an anonymous clean-room build.

  4. November 2026

    Graph coloring · known lower bound

    Could 42 dots avoid a one-color group of five?

    Join every pair of dots with a red or blue line. In one known coloring, checking all 850,668 groups of five found none whose lines are all the same color. A separate Lean proof formalized the result.

    Read the original evidence and scope

    The browser checker checks the known 42-vertex construction across 850,668 five-vertex subsets. A separate Lean formalization then checked the exact lower-bound target in 27 chunks and a final composition.

    Established: a Lean-checked formalization of the known R(5,5) ≥ 43 witness, using a warm local cache. Not established: a new construction, the exact Ramsey number, a clean-checkout replay, or dossier acceptance.

    Read the construction and checker scope →
  5. November 18, 2026

    Collatz · checking the same proof twice

    Two runs agreed on all 363 proof files

    Both runs completed the mathematical checks. The first run’s outer timer expired afterwards. The second finished normally. Their agreement was an engineering check on a bounded Collatz proof.

    Read the original evidence and scope

All per-module source, output, and exit-code hashes agree between the runs. The first Docker wrapper ended with 124 after its watchdog expired; the second returned 0.

    Established by this November 18 replay: agreement between the two recorded runs in the pinned environment. Not established by this replay: an independent operator rebuild. A fresh public-clone replay passed on November 27; third-party mathematical review remains open.

  6. November 18, 2026

    Fractions · exact arithmetic

    Which fractions split into three unit fractions?

    A unit fraction has 1 on top. For instance, 4/21 equals 1/6 + 1/63 + 1/126. For a filtered list of 831,860 candidate denominators through ten million, the campaign recorded an exact decomposition for each one.

    Read the original evidence and scope

    The fixed factor-conditioned candidate set has one exact witness per recorded candidate. All 427 bounded base-search misses were resolved within the recorded cap. The miss analysis found no single pattern covering the cases and no new identity family.

    Established: checked finite identities for this filtered set and range. Not established: a decomposition for every integer or a proof of the Erdős–Straus conjecture.

  8. November 24, 2026

    Collatz · checked proof

    How long can a qualifying start take to fall?

    Start at 3 or above. In the first 23 shortcut moves, count how often the move begins on an odd number. At most 14? Lean checked that the sequence falls below its start within 37 ordinary steps.

    Read the original evidence and scope

    The theorem covers starts n ≥ 3 whose first 23 accelerated inputs contain at most 14 odd values. Lean counts exactly 7,507,638 of 8,388,608 residue classes under that condition. Three nearby parameter checks remain exploratory.

    Established: the exact bounded statement, its residue count, and the recorded Lean receipt. Not established: eventual descent for every integer, a smaller combined proof basis, or the Collatz conjecture.

    Read the theorem, receipt, and limits →

    Collatz · correction to a calculation record

    Which instructions went into the 34-move count?

    A finite calculation followed selected odd starting numbers for up to 34 moves from one odd number to the next. A later check identified the exact text files used by the two original calculations. It repaired the paper trail without repeating the calculation.

    Read the original evidence and scope

    The step-34 finite calculation and independent recomputation were already recorded as passing. The package had failed text hygiene because its Markdown protocol inputs used CRLF. A byte-binding audit now connects the preserved original bytes to the hashes recorded by both runs; it did not rerun the calculation.

    Established: exact input-byte provenance for the existing receipts and preservation of the packaging failure. Not established: class-wide descent, later behavior, or Collatz convergence.
