MathCase research roadmap

Where does the math go next?

A coloring can answer a question about one graph. A proof can explain why an answer holds for every graph of a given kind. The open work depends on which kind of answer each question needs.

What remains open?

Collatz sequencesWhich starting numbers are guaranteed to drop?

Lean checked a drop by step 37 for every start of at least 3 with at most 14 odd inputs in its first 23 shortcut moves. Exactly 7,507,638 of 8,388,608 parity classes meet this condition. A separate computer count combined five conditions. The published check does not prove a combined descent theorem for the five, or that every sequence reaches 1.

Graphs & coloringsCould a different coloring work where this one fails?

The known 42-dot coloring for R(5,5) has a saved Lean check. Independent review and a build from a fresh checkout remain open. For R(6,6), a computer checked a 101-dot coloring and ruled out adding a dot while keeping every old line fixed. That leaves other arrangements open.

FractionsWhich denominators admit a single formula?

The fraction search recorded 831,860 exact examples in a filtered set. Separate algebraic formulas explain whole families, including numbers of the form 28k + 21. A formula covering every integer n ≥ 2 remains the open Erdős–Straus question.

Checking researchCan someone else reproduce the result, and does reusing a proof help?

Two saved runs of the 363-module Collatz proof agreed. Rebuilding that historical run from a fresh public checkout still needs its exact environment. Separately, one matched comparison of proof attempts with and without saved context tied on every test group. Whether it helps on average remains open.

Future subjects and explanations

The longer-term aim is to make more areas of mathematics approachable at different levels. Geometry, calculus and probability are examples of areas beyond the current guided case catalog. New entries would need a specific question, a worked explanation and a record of what was checked.

Today you can read the prime-divisibility argument at five levels on this site. The source also describes five historical drafts for a theorem about lists. Independent teaching review and evidence about what readers learned remain open.
