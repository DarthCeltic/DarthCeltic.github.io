A bounded result, checked from source twice

Collatz: when does a number get smaller?

For 2,652 checked number patterns, the value after 32 standard steps is smaller than the start. This proves a bounded rule for those listed patterns; it does not prove that every Collatz sequence reaches 1.

When do 32 Collatz steps take you downhill?

Plain statement: if exactly 12 of the first 20 accelerated inputs are odd, the value after 32 ordinary Collatz steps is smaller than the start. Lean proves this for every qualifying residue progression, with every natural parameter.

125,970 of the 1,048,576 residues modulo 2²⁰ have exactly 12 odd inputs; for every such r and k, C³²(2²⁰k+r) = 531,441k + A²⁰(r) < 2²⁰k+r.

An accelerated step halves an even input. For an odd input, it applies (3n+1)/2. The parity condition makes 20 accelerated steps correspond to 32 ordinary steps. Lean proves the count by a recurrence on sibling residue classes, then ties that exact count to the same parity predicate used by the descent theorem.

Limits: this covers a fixed parity pattern, not every starting number. It gives no eventual-return rule, proves no global convergence, and makes no priority claim. The count is a residue-class density, not a percentage of Collatz solved.

What if twelve or fewer inputs are odd?

Plain statement: for every starting number n ≥ 3, if at most 12 of its first 20 accelerated Collatz inputs are odd, then one specific ordinary iterate is smaller than n.

Let h be the odd-input count. Lean proves C^(20+h)(n) < n for 0 ≤ h ≤ 12; the checked step is between 20 and 32.

The qualifying condition covers exactly 910,596 of 1,048,576 residue classes modulo 2²⁰ (86.8412%). Every number at least 3 in those classes has the stated drop. This gives a lower bound on the natural density of starts that drop at one of steps 20 through 32; it does not claim a drop at step 32 for all of them.

Why the uniform cutoff stops at twelve odd inputs

For any residue with exactly 13 odd inputs in the first 20 accelerated steps, Lean proves that some member of its full residue family is not smaller at ordinary step 33. This rules out extending the all-members guarantee to every 13-odd family at that endpoint; it does not say every 13-odd start fails to descend.

Limits: the step depends on the odd-input count. This is bounded descent, not a convergence argument, and it makes no novelty or priority claim. The starts 1 and 2 are excluded: their step-30 values are not smaller than their starts.

What if thirteen or fewer inputs are odd?

Plain statement: for every start n ≥ 3, if at most 13 of its first 21 accelerated Collatz inputs are odd, then the ordinary iterate at step 21+h is smaller than n, where h is the odd-input count.

1,898,712 / 2,097,152 residue classes modulo 2²¹ qualify (90.5376%). The proved endpoint is between steps 21 and 34, depending on h.

Lean lifts the result to every 2²¹k+r ≥ 3 whose residue has at most 13 odd inputs. A separate boundary theorem proves that each residue with exactly 14 odd inputs has a representative 2²¹+r that is not smaller at step 35.

Limits: this is a parity-conditioned first drop. The 90.5376% is a share of residue classes, not a percentage of Collatz solved. It proves no global convergence and makes no novelty claim.

A second parity pattern that guarantees a first drop

Start with the shortcut: for an even number, halve it. For an odd number, calculate 3n+1 and halve once. Repeat that rule 22 times.

Plain statement: for every start n ≥ 3, if no more than 13 of those 22 shortcut moves begin on odd numbers, a regular Collatz step takes the number below its start by step 35.

If h of the 22 shortcut moves begin on odd numbers, the exact claim is C^(22+h)(n) < n whenever h ≤ 13. An odd shortcut move represents two regular steps; an even move represents one.

Lean proves the claim for exactly 3,593,934 of 4,194,304 possible 22-step parity patterns (85.6861%). This is a separate family from the 20- and 21-step results: their pattern sets are not nested, so the percentages are not a direct improvement comparison.

Limits: this is a parity-conditioned first drop, not a rule for every input. It does not show that every sequence reaches 1, solve Collatz, or make a novelty claim.

At most 17 odd inputs in 27 shortcut moves imply a drop by step 44.

The rule: for an even number, halve it. For an odd number, triple it, add 1, and halve once. Repeat this 27 times.

What Lean checked: for every start n ≥ 26,018, if no more than 17 of those 27 moves start on odd numbers, the ordinary sequence falls below its starting value at step 27+h, where h is the number of odd moves. That is between steps 27 and 44.

For example, 26,018 has 12 odd moves; it falls below its start after 39 ordinary steps and is then 107. This example illustrates the rule; the Lean proof checks the general statement.

Limit: this covers starts meeting the stated size and parity conditions. It does not show that every number eventually reaches 1 or solve the Collatz conjecture.

Lean checked · bounded first drop

At most 14 odd shortcut moves give a first drop by step 37.

Plain statement: repeat this shortcut 23 times: halve an even number; for an odd number, calculate 3n+1 and halve once. For every start n ≥ 3, if at most 14 shortcut moves begin on odd numbers, the ordinary Collatz sequence is below its start after 23+h steps, where h is the number of odd moves.

Lean checked exactly 7,507,638 of 8,388,608 residue classes modulo 2²³. Since h ≤ 14, the proved drop occurs no later than ordinary step 37.

This is one precise bounded-descent rule. It does not show that every number eventually reaches 1, prove the Collatz conjecture, or establish novelty.

Lean source replay · exact scope

The proved claim

Plain statement: take any one of the 2,652 exact coefficient pairs in the linked source and any natural number k. Starting from 2²⁰k+r, exactly 32 standard Collatz steps reach 3¹²k+q, and that endpoint is strictly smaller than the start.

∀ (r,q) ∈ S, ∀ k ∈ ℕ: C³²(2²⁰k+r) = 3¹²k+q ∧ 3¹²k+q < 2²⁰k+r

Here S is the finite set of 2,652 pairs listed in the theorem source. The map C sends an even number to half of it and an odd number to three times the number plus one. The theorem proves the trajectory equality and the strict decrease together for every natural k.

What this does not prove: it does not cover every starting number, give a descent rule for all residue classes, or show that every trajectory reaches 1. It is not a solution of the Collatz conjecture and makes no novelty claim.

Exact integer consequence

Why the recorded endpoint is smaller

Plain statement: for every listed pair, the starting expression exceeds the endpoint expression by a positive amount for every natural k.

(2²⁰k+r) − (3¹²k+q) = 517,135k + (r−q)

The source-pair audit checks all 2,652 intercepts r−q. Every one is positive; the minimum is 117. Since k ≥ 0 and the coefficient of k is positive, the displayed gap is positive. This algebra explains the inequality, while the Lean replay establishes that 32 Collatz steps actually reach the stated endpoint.

How much of the residue space is covered?

This theorem covers 2,652 listed infinite progressions; it does not itself imply a density statement. A separate finite audit records certificates for 1,021,248 of 1,048,576 remainders modulo 2²⁰ (about 97.394%). That is a finite share of one residue space, not a share of all natural numbers and not a percentage of the conjecture solved. Read that separate audit.
