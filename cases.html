When do 32 Collatz steps take a number downhill?

Plain claim: if exactly 12 of a number’s first 20 accelerated Collatz inputs are odd, its value after 32 ordinary steps is smaller than its start. This holds for every number in each qualifying residue progression.

125,970 of 1,048,576 residues satisfy the condition; Lean proves the exact count and the descent for every progression member.

The result covers a fixed parity pattern, not every starting value; it proves no global convergence and makes no priority claim.

Do twelve or fewer odd inputs lead to a drop?

Plain claim: for every start at least 3, if no more than 12 of its first 20 accelerated inputs are odd, one ordinary iterate at step 20 through 32 is smaller than the start.

910,596 of 1,048,576 residue classes qualify. The exact step is 20 plus the odd-input count.

The Lean theorem covers every member of each qualifying progression. A separate 13-odd theorem marks the limit of this all-members guarantee at its corresponding endpoint. No convergence or novelty claim is made.

Can 22 parity steps guarantee a first drop?

Plain claim: repeat this shortcut 22 times: halve even numbers; for odd numbers, do 3n+1 and halve. For every start n ≥ 3 whose shortcut path begins on an odd number at most 13 times, a regular Collatz step takes it below its start by step 35.

The Lean-checked count is 3,593,934 of 4,194,304 possible 22-step parity patterns (85.6861%).

Example: starting at 7, the shortcut path begins 7 → 11 → 17 → 26 → 13 → 20 → 10 → 5 → 8 → 4 → 2 → 1. This is one example, not the proof. The theorem covers every qualifying start, but does not prove every Collatz sequence reaches 1 or make a novelty claim. Its pattern set differs from the 20- and 21-step results.

Collatz · Lean checked

Can a number fall below itself within 44 steps?

The rule: halve an even number. For an odd number, triple it, add 1, and halve once. Repeat 27 times.

Plain claim: for every start n ≥ 26,018 with at most 17 odd moves, the ordinary Collatz value at step 27+h is below its start, no later than step 44.

Example: 26,018 has 12 odd moves and falls below its start after 39 ordinary steps, reaching 107. That example illustrates the rule; it is not the proof.

The rule covers only starts that meet its size and parity conditions. It does not prove that every Collatz sequence reaches 1.

Can 23 shortcut moves force a first drop?

Plain claim: for every start n ≥ 3, if at most 14 of the first 23 shortcut moves begin on an odd number, a regular Collatz step takes it below its start at step 23+h, no later than step 37.

Lean counted exactly 7,507,638 of 8,388,608 residue classes modulo 2²³. The descent theorem reports [propext, Quot.sound]; the count and combined theorem report [Classical.choice, propext, Quot.sound]. No sorryAx was reported.

Levent Alpöge published the Claude-assisted proof; the Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative coordinated independent checks. The result and proof belong to the upstream authors and community, not MathCase.

MathCase’s small checker pins four public source files and verifies their byte counts and SHA-256 values. It does not download the proof export, elaborate the target, replay the proof, or independently validate the theorem.
