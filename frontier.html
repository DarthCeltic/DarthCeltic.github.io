Frontier mathematics

Which patterns can you avoid?

Color numbers on a line, join dots with colored edges, or split a fraction into smaller pieces. A small example gives you the rules of each puzzle. The six checks below show what happens when you take those rules further.

Number patterns · two colors

Can two colors avoid seven equally spaced numbers of one color?

Write 2, 5, 8. The gap is always 3. Keep going and you get 11, 14, 17, 20. Coloring all seven blue would make exactly the pattern you want to avoid.

A known coloring assigns two colors to 1 through 3,703 and avoids every such seven-number pattern. MathCase checked it. One entire family of ways to color 3,704 positions failed. Other families are still possible.

Inspect the exact result, its limits and sources

These numbers can skip positions: for example, 2, 5, and 8 are equally spaced. A checked two-coloring covers 3,703 positions without a single-color pattern of seven. That reproduces the known lower bound W(2,7) ≥ 3,704.

MathCase also checked 617 cases in one specified quadratic-residue construction family at length 3,704, covering 644,907,008 assignments. That family is ruled out; other colorings are not.

Next question. check a different construction family, or produce a general certificate covering every coloring at length 3,704.

Number patterns · five colors

Can five colors avoid even a three-number pattern?

Give 2 and 5 the same color. Now 8 needs a different color because the gaps in 2, 5, 8 are equal. That is just one of the triples a coloring must handle.

A checked coloring works through 170. At 171 the computer search ran for 20 seconds and returned “unknown.” A coloring or a proof ruling all of them out would settle this next step.

Inspect the exact result, its limits and sources

A checked coloring covers 170 positions without a monochromatic three-term progression, so W(5,3) ≥ 171. The unrestricted 171-position search has 855 variables and 38,006 clauses.

The 20-second solver probe ended unknown; it did not prove that 171 is impossible. A small W(2,3)=9 positive control was independently checked.

Next question. obtain a checked result for the unrestricted 171-position formula, or find an avoiding coloring there.

Addition · five colors

Where can you put 208 without creating a same-color sum?

If 2 and 3 are blue, 5 cannot also be blue, because 2 + 3 = 5. Only sums of two distinct positive numbers count.

Two known colorings get through 207. MathCase tried all five colors for 208 and allowed up to 18 earlier numbers to change color. Neither starting arrangement could extend that way. A more extensive rearrangement could still work.

MathCase also tested one near-miss of its own: it had three conflicts, and a Z3 solver reported no repair using ten or fewer changes. That result applies only to this saved arrangement; another coloring could still work.

Inspect the exact result, its limits and sources

The rule forbids same-color sums a + b = c when a and b are distinct positive numbers. Two known colorings cover 1 through 207; MathCase checked both and searched every color for 208 while changing up to 18 old entries.

All ten bounded searches finished. Starting from either published coloring, at least 19 old positions must change before an extension can work. A coloring farther from both starting points could still exist.

For a separate MathCase near-miss, the solver encoded all 10,712 allowed sums and reported no valid repair within ten changes. This is a bounded solver report, not a proof certificate or a claim that 208 is impossible.

On a different saved coloring with one conflict (4 + 8 = 12), Z3 reported no repair within three changes. A separate exhaustive check examined all 4,064,044 edits that could change that conflict, using all 10,712 allowed sums, and found no repair. The solver output has no proof certificate; both results apply only to this saved arrangement, not every coloring of 1 through 208.

Next question. find a valid 208 coloring outside those neighborhoods, or check a proof that covers every five-coloring.

Graphs · four colors

Can 51 dots avoid a one-color triangle?

Draw three dots and join each pair. Three red lines give a red triangle. Change any one line to blue and that triangle disappears. With many dots, that change affects other triangles too.

A known four-color drawing on 50 dots passes the triangle check. Keeping those lines fixed, every way of adding a 51st dot creates a one-color triangle. Even changing one or two old lines is insufficient for this drawing.

Inspect the exact result, its limits and sources

A known graph on 50 vertices has no monochromatic triangle, giving R(3,3,3,3) ≥ 51. MathCase checked every triangle and every way to attach a 51st vertex while leaving the old graph unchanged.

A second bounded check found that an extension from this particular starting graph needs at least three old edges recolored. The published interval is 51–60; MathCase did not reproduce the upper-bound proof.

Next question. search for a genuinely different 50-vertex starting graph or expand the certified class of possible starts.

Graphs · two colors

Can you add a 102nd dot without making a one-color group of six?

Six dots have 15 lines between them. If all 15 are red, those dots form a red group of six. The same test applies to blue.

A known drawing on 101 dots avoids both kinds of group. Adding a dot while keeping the old coloring fails. A checked restriction says that any extension of this particular drawing must change at least 16 old lines.

Inspect the exact result, its limits and sources

The known Paley graph on 101 vertices has no monochromatic six-clique, giving R(6,6) ≥ 102. A checked certificate rules out adding one new vertex while keeping every old edge the same.

A separate Lean source replay now checks the existence statement for this same 101-vertex witness. It confirms the known lower bound; it does not add a new bound. The public clean-checkout replay remains open because the pinned image is not verified as anonymously pullable.

Other bounded checks find that at least 16 old edges must change. None of 33 predefined structured edge-change patterns gave a valid extension; each produced a six-clique. These restrictions apply to this starting graph and these changes only.

Next question. find a materially different 102-vertex coloring and check every six-vertex subset.

Fractions · exact identities

Can you write 4/21 as three fractions with 1 on top?

Try 1/6 + 1/63 + 1/126. In parts of size 1/126, the sum is 21 + 2 + 1 = 24 parts. And 24/126 is exactly 4/21.

Lean checked two formulas that work for denominators 21, 49, 77 and every later number obtained by adding 28. Another formula covers all positive multiples of seven. The full Erdős–Straus question asks for every integer denominator of at least 2.

Yes. Two exact examples are 4/21 = 1/7 + 1/22 + 1/462 and 4/21 = 1/6 + 1/63 + 1/126. The checked formulas extend to every number of the form 28k+21.

A separate formula covers every positive multiple of seven. These are exact identities for specific infinite families, not a solution of the Erdős–Straus conjecture for every integer.
